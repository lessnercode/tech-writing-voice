# Technical writing in the author's voice

Produce specific, technically sound prose that sounds like the author talking to a peer. Use the author's evidence and opinions. Keep the writing direct and useful.

## 1. Gather the brief, sources and voice samples

Read the supplied material before writing. Separate voice samples from factual sources and the draft being edited.

Establish the audience, format, purpose and approximate length. State the central idea in one sentence before drafting. Ask a focused question when missing context could change the technical meaning, angle or voice.

Read [references/voice-matching.md](references/voice-matching.md) and calibrate against the author's samples. Prefer samples in the target format and apply explicit corrections from the author. When samples are missing, ask for them. Use a provisional direct practitioner voice only if the author chooses to proceed.

Read [references/style-guide.md](references/style-guide.md) before drafting or editing. Apply its full banned lists and rules to authored prose. Preserve exact code, commands, identifiers and source quotations needed for accuracy.

## 2. Verify technical claims before writing

Query a local technical knowledge base first, if one exists. For example, an author's `wiki-doc-sync` skill may provide sourced answers. Use the tools actually available, then inspect the underlying documentation where freshness or scope matters.

Check product behavior against primary documentation, release notes or supplied technical notes. Verify statistics against their original sources. Record the applicable version, date and conditions when they affect the claim. Treat voice samples as evidence of style, without assuming their factual claims are current.

Keep a compact working record of each material claim and its supporting source. Distinguish documented behavior, supplied firsthand experience, opinion and inference. Label an inference when readers need that distinction.

Never invent numbers, quotations, sources, links or firsthand experience. Keep conclusions within the evidence. If a claim can't be verified, ask for the missing evidence, remove it or narrow it to what the sources support. Keep unresolved issues separate from publishable copy and identify them when delivering a draft.

Resolve conflicting sources before relying on them. If access is blocked, report what couldn't be checked. A plausible answer or a search snippet doesn't establish a claim.

## 3. Choose the angle and draft

Open with something specific enough to earn the read. Useful openings include a supported counterintuitive claim, a firsthand observation, a real technical tension or a verified number. Supply only the context the reader needs.

For a short post, move through the stakes, mechanism and practical implication. Land the point or ask a specific question worth debating. Use connected paragraphs. Use lists when the material calls for steps or comparison.

For a blog post or article, give the mechanism and evidence enough room. Choose sections around the reader's questions. When the audience includes practitioners and decision makers, connect technical behavior to operational or business consequences.

Match length to substance. A short observation can be a text post. A deployment walkthrough may need a sequence. An evergreen comparison may need an article. Suggest a better format when useful and follow the requested deliverable.

Give LinkedIn posts enough substance to stand alone. By default, provide optional source links as a separate first-comment draft. Keep hashtags optional, with three at most, placed at the end. Respect the author's requested citation placement. Blog posts and articles can carry source links beside the claims they support.

Use education and supported opinion for practitioner content. Use sales framing when the brief calls for it. Keep opinions consistent with the author's expressed views and accept redlines as further voice evidence.

## 4. Self-edit against the style reference

Run a separate editing pass using [references/style-guide.md](references/style-guide.md), including its punctuation, formatting and self-commentary rules.

Remove banned wording and sentence patterns. Make each point once, in its strongest position. Vary sentence and paragraph length naturally. Cut generic openers, recap endings and repeated claims.

Use zero em dashes. Default to no bold in narrative paragraphs. Cut prose that announces its own argument, structure or honesty. Keep material qualifications and provenance attached to the claims they limit.

Compare the draft with the voice samples again. Check the opening, sentence rhythm and degree of certainty. Remove humor, personal stories or rhetorical habits unsupported by the samples. Reread technical statements after every substantive rewrite.

## 5. Final checklist and delivery

Read and complete [references/editing-checklist.md](references/editing-checklist.md).

Deliver the requested copy in its intended format. Keep research notes, questions and any first-comment links separate. Include a brief unresolved-claims note only when something still needs the author's attention. A draft with unresolved factual claims must be identified as needing verification.

The copy is ready when it matches the available voice evidence, follows the style reference and has support for every material factual claim. Every supplied link must have been opened and checked, or clearly reported as unverified outside the copy. Never describe unchecked copy as ready to publish.
