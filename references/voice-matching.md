# Voice matching

## Gather evidence

Read the author's samples before drafting. Two to five representative pieces usually provide a useful starting point. Prefer recent work the author still endorses and include the requested format when available. Transcripts can reveal phrasing, but spoken repetition often needs editing for print.

Separate writing samples from reference material. A vendor document can establish a product behavior. It doesn't establish the author's voice. A heavily edited guest post may be a weaker voice sample than an ordinary post the author wrote alone.

Ask which sample sounds most like the author if the samples disagree. Treat redlines and explicit preferences as stronger evidence than a repeated habit the author now dislikes.

## Build a compact voice profile

Record observable choices with a short example from the supplied samples:

| Feature | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Openings | An observation, a concrete event, a number or a question grounded in a real tension |
| Rhythm | Sentence length, fragments and how paragraphs build an idea |
| Diction | Everyday verbs, technical terms, contractions and words the author avoids |
| Point of view | First person, second person or impersonal explanation |
| Stance | How directly the author judges a tradeoff and how they bound certainty |
| Explanation | How much mechanism, context and evidence the reader gets |
| Personality | Humor, asides or frustration actually present in the samples |
| Endings | An operational consequence, a decision or a specific open question |
| Formatting | Headings, lists and emphasis that serve a repeated structure |

Use the profile to choose phrasing. Don't turn it into a rigid paragraph formula. A conference-peer register is a useful fallback only when the author has agreed to proceed without samples.

## Match without inventing

Borrow patterns rather than copying distinctive sentences. Keep the author's technical vocabulary and level of familiarity with the audience. Use the style guide to remove banned wording and structures even when they appear in a sample.

Carry over opinions only when the author has supplied them or endorsed them in the samples. If a draft needs a position the author hasn't expressed, ask a focused question. Don't convert neutral notes into a confident personal stance.

Use first person for experience the author actually supplied. Never invent a customer conversation, a deployment, a career event or a personal memory to make an opening sound authentic. A documented example can be described without claiming the author lived it.

Keep humor proportional to the evidence. Don't assign an outgoing, sarcastic or combative personality. Preserve the degree of formality the samples support while applying the style reference's punctuation and clarity rules.

## Calibrate across formats

- LinkedIn posts can carry one observation with a concrete implication. Keep the author's normal phrasing without manufacturing suspense or chopping every sentence onto a new line.
- Blog posts can spend more time on mechanisms and examples. Keep the same vocabulary and stance as the short-form samples while allowing fuller paragraphs.
- Articles can develop a longer argument with sections where needed. Let evidence determine the space each section gets.

## Compare the draft with the samples

Read the opening, a technical explanation and the close beside comparable passages from the samples. Check whether the word choice, confidence and rhythm belong to the same person.

Read the draft aloud or review it for spoken cadence. Rewrite lines that could belong to anyone. Remove exaggerated mannerisms and copied phrases. Confirm that edits for voice haven't changed the technical meaning.

If samples are unavailable, ask for them before claiming a match. If the author chooses a provisional voice, state that limitation outside the copy. Use subsequent corrections to improve the profile.
